#title
The Possessed Woman and the Silver Fox

#summary
A mischievous silver fox living near Tower Mountain in China possesses a neighboring village woman and refuses to leave until confronted by a local farmer armed with his ox-whip. The defiant spirit is finally subdued when the farmer cracks his whip and commands the entity to flee.

#details
In the region southwest of Jiaozhou Bay in China, Tower Mountain was known for an old temple dedicated to the Old Mother and a nearby cave inhabited by talking silver foxes. One such fox frequently tricked travelers by speaking to them in human voices and wearing disguises made of leaves and plants. After a local farmer named Wang struck the fox with his ox-whip during one of its pranks, the fox vowed vengeance and subsequently possessed a woman in a neighboring village. The woman's family attempted various exorcisms, including hanging a Taoist priest's picture and threatening to call upon the God of War, but the fox boasted that it feared only Wang from the Eastern village. Summoned to help, Wang arrived with his ox-whip, shouted at the hidden entity, and snapped his whip, causing the possessed woman to be freed as the fox hissed and fled out the window.

#location
Tower Mountain, located southwest of Jiaozhou Bay in Shandong Province, China.

#upstream-source
*The Chinese Fairy Book*, edited by R. Wilhelm and translated by Frederick H. Martens (1921).

#title
The River-Divide Curse of Wushan and Yuepu

#summary
A 300-year-old inter-village curse declared that any marriage across a narrow stream dividing two communities would bring lifelong misfortune, perpetuating a bitter multi-generational feud. The curse was finally broken when a young couple defied the ban, married, and had healthy twin boys, prompting both villages to officially abolish the ancient prohibition.

#details
For over three centuries, a narrow stream barely a meter wide stood as an uncrossable social barrier separating the villages of Wushan and Yuepu in Fujian province, China. Originating from a bloody 300-year-old dispute over water rights and later escalated by fighting over ancestral graves, a rigid ban on inter-village marriage took root. Passed down through generations, the local belief dictated that anyone who dared marry across the stream would be visited by a curse of persistent misfortune. 

When a young woman from Yuepu and a young man from Wushan fell deeply in love, their families strictly opposed the match, citing the generational curse. The couple defied their families and married regardless, though the severe backlash and fear of the curse forced them to flee 1,500 kilometers away to another province. However, when the woman later gave birth to twin boys who brought joy and prosperity to the family, the villagers' superstitious dread dissolved. Recognizing that the curse had failed to manifest, the communities held a formal reconciliation ceremony, abolishing the centuries-old ban.

#location
The neighboring villages of Wushan and Yuepu, separated by a stream in Fujian province, China.
