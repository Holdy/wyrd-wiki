The Phantom Manor of Knighton Gorges

Summary

A magnificent stately home dogged by an ancient crime and persistent tragedy was pulled down by its owner to break a dark curse, yet the phantom house still materialises every New Year's Eve to the sound of spectral music and midnight carriage wheels.

Details

Knighton Gorges was considered one of the finest manor houses on the Isle of Wight, but it carried a heavy burden of misfortune from its earliest days. One of its early owners, Hugh de Morville, was among the four knights who assassinated Thomas Becket in 1170, a deed that allegedly brought a lasting curse upon the estate. Over the centuries, the property played host to deep tragedies, including the death of Sir Theobald Russell from battle wounds and his wife dying of a broken heart in the notorious 'Room of Tears'. Later, in July 1721, the owner Tristram Dillington died under suspicious circumstances; to save his estate from forfeiture following his suspected suicide, his body was allegedly strapped to his horse and driven into a pond.

In 1821, the final owner, Maurice George Bisset, had the grand mansion entirely demolished—either driven half-mad by personal misfortune and sickness or determined to prevent his daughter from inheriting a place so thoroughly dogged by sorrow. Despite the physical house being reduced to little more than a pair of stone gateposts, local folklore maintains that the curse cannot be completely undone. Every New Year's Eve, the phantom manor reappears in the dead of night complete with brightly lit windows, a ghostly Georgian party, harpsichord music, and the sound of carriage wheels before vanishing as the clock strikes midnight.
