    The Gibbet on Kettlestone Common

    Summary

    In 1792, after being convicted of murder, a local man's executed body was suspended in an iron cage on Kettlestone Common where it remained on public display for nearly forty years. This grim spectacle drew curious onlookers from across the region and left a lasting mark on local history and parish geography.

    Details

    In January 1792, Sarah Cusher of Kettlestone fell gravely ill and died after being given poison by William Anthony, who sought to conceal her pregnancy. Following a swift trial at the Thetford Assizes in March 1792, Anthony was found guilty of murder and sentenced to execution. In accordance with the Murder Act of 1751, judges frequently ordered that the bodies of heinous criminals be either dissected or "hung in chains" near the scene of their crime. After Anthony was executed, his remains were placed inside a specially manufactured iron gibbet cage and transported back to Kettlestone Common near Clipstone. The macabre display attracted significant attention, being noted in contemporary journals and diaries, such as that of Norfolk diarist Mary Hardy. Anthony’s skeletal remains hung exposed on the common until the gibbet was finally dismantled around 1830, with parts of the structure reportedly repurposed for the local church porch gates.
