    The Sledgehammered Memorial of St Mary's Church

    Summary

    Following the outbreak of Britain's longest school strike in Burston, a grieving father smashed his fallen son's church memorial with a sledgehammer in defiance of the local rector. Today, the cracked plaque remains permanently fixed to the wall of St Mary's Church as a lasting testament to the bitter class divides of the village.

    Details

    In 1913, Tom and Kitty Higdon, Christian Socialist teachers at the village school in Burston, came into bitter conflict with the rector, Reverend Charles Tucker Eland, and the school managers after Tom was elected to the parish council and championed better wages and conditions for local farmworkers. When the Higdons were unjustly dismissed, the villagers rallied in protest, pulling their children out of the parish school to form an open-air protest on the village green that evolved into the Burston Strike School—the longest strike in British history, lasting 25 years. The conflict tore the rural community apart, pitting parishioners against the church establishment. During World War I, a local young man named Herbert Garnham was killed on the Somme in September 1916 at the age of 25. An uncle erected a memorial tablet to him inside St Mary's Church. However, Herbert’s father, a steadfast participant in the strike who despised the rector and refused to allow church involvement in commemorating his son, stormed the church with a sledgehammer, smashed the memorial to the floor, and was subsequently sentenced to a month in prison. The shattered plaque was repaired and rehung, and visitors to the church can still see the visible cracks running across it to this day.
