#title
Scratching Fanny of Stoke Ferry

#summary
Following the tragic death of his wife at a village inn, a Norfolk money lender fled to London with his sister-in-law, ultimately igniting one of the most famous poltergeist hoaxes in British history. Their time running the rural establishment preceded the notorious "Scratching Fanny" hauntings in Cock Lane.

#details
Around 1756, William Kent, a money lender from Norfolk, married Elizabeth Lynes and moved to Stoke Ferry, where he operated the Crown Inn—located at the corner of what is now the High Street and Lynn Road. After Elizabeth tragically died following childbirth, William began a relationship with Elizabeth's sister, Frances (known as Fanny). Because contemporary Anglican canon law prohibited a widower from marrying his deceased wife's sister, the couple abandoned the inn and moved to Cock Lane in Smithfield, London, to live together. While lodging there and embroiled in a financial dispute with their landlord, Richard Parsons, Fanny tragically contracted smallpox and died while four months pregnant. Following her death, Parsons and his 12-year-old daughter orchestrated an elaborate fraud by claiming that Fanny's ghost was haunting the property with mysterious knocking and scratching noises. The sensational case, which came to be known as the Cock Lane ghost or "Scratching Fanny," drew massive public attention, brought false accusations of murder against William Kent, and sparked national debate before a public commission exposed the hoax and imprisoned Parsons.

#location
The 16th-century building of the former Crown Inn situated at the corner of High Street and Lynn Road in Stoke Ferry, Norfolk.
