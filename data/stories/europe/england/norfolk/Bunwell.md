#title
Kindred Spirits

#summary
An author living in a Norfolk farmhouse is contacted by a friendly phantom seeking to right a 1940s family injustice involving missing estate funds. Through guidance pointing to the local churchyard, she uncovers the ghost's true history and incorporates his tale into her novel.

#details
After moving into a farmhouse in Bunwell, author Lucy McCarraher was informed by a psychic friend during a thunderstorm that an unseen visitor wished to speak with her. The spirit claimed he had been falsely accused during the 1940s of spiriting away family funds after a wealthy relative died without leaving a will, causing him and his family to be driven out of the village by bitter disputes. The ghost directed them to his grave in the village churchyard, where they successfully verified his existence. Grateful for the chance to set the record straight, the phantom also advised Lucy on where to position her workspace to write her novels before departing for good.

#location
The parish churchyard in Bunwell, Norfolk, houses the grave of the phantom who revealed his 1940s family dispute.

*Kindred Spirits* by Lucy McCarraher (published by YouWriteOn/Legend Press).

#title
The Christened Cockerel of Bunwell

#summary
In August 1465, villagers in Bunwell gathered to summon an aerial spirit in search of buried treasure, famously baptizing a cockerel as a Christian to fulfill the spirit's demand for a sacrifice.

#details
On a Sunday night in August 1465, a group of villagers in Bunwell, Norfolk—led by John Cans and Robert Hikkes—gathered to perform a ritual summoning of an "aerial spirit" in the hope of discovering hidden treasure. The spirit revealed through a mirror that great riches lay buried just a couple of miles away beneath a burial mound in the neighboring village of Forncett, but demanded a "Christian body" as a sacrificial burnt offering. Seeking a loophole, the ringleaders procured a cockerel, assembled godfathers and godmothers, baptized the bird with holy water, and gave it a Christian name. Having thus created a "Christian body" out of a fowl, they sacrificed the christened cockerel to the spirit. Content with the offering, the spirit directed them to Nonmete Hill in Forncett, where the treasure hunters successfully dug up one hundred shillings.

#location
The village of Bunwell, Norfolk, and Nonmete Hill in the neighboring parish of Forncett.

#title
The Spirit of Nonmete Hill

#summary
Two men summoned an aerial spirit in Bunwell, promising a human sacrifice in exchange for the secret location of a buried treasure hoard.

#details
In 1465, according to historical jury findings, two men named John Cans and Robert Hikkes performed a ritual in Bunwell to summon a spirit of the air. They made a dark pact with the supernatural entity, promising to sacrifice the body of a Christian man if the spirit would reveal where a hidden hoard of wealth was buried. Directing them to Nonmete Hill, the spirit led the men to uncover ancient coins valued at one hundred shillings upon digging into the mound.

#location
Nonmete Hill is located in the Bunwell area where Hill Farm now stands at grid reference TM1158942.

#title
The Treasure Seekers and the Spirit of the Air

#summary
In 1465, two men summoned an aerial spirit and promised a human sacrifice in exchange for the location of a buried treasure hoard at Nonmete Hill. Upon digging where directed, they successfully uncovered a stash of ancient coins.

#details
According to the findings of a 1465 jury impanelled by the King's escheator for Norfolk, two men named John Cans and Robert Hikkes engaged in folk magic in Bunwell. They called up a spirit of the air and promised to sacrifice the body of a Christian man if the entity would reveal hidden treasure. The spirit directed them to Nonmete Hill, where the men dug into the earth and unearthed a hoard of Roman coins valued at 100 shillings.

#location
Nonmete Hill, historically located where Hill Farm now stands near Bunwell and Forncett in south Norfolk.

#upstream-source
Augustus Jessopp: 'Hill-Digging and Magic' in 'The Nineteenth Century' (Vol. 4, Jan. 1887); Augustus Jessopp: 'Random Roaming' (T. Fisher Unwin, 1896).
