#title
The Brandiston Witch Informant

#summary
In folkloric field interviews conducted in the early twentieth century, a local Brandiston resident firmly maintained the traditional reality of witchcraft, grounding his belief in biblical accounts.

#details
When folklorist Mark Taylor conducted field investigations into traditional customs, folk medicine, and witchcraft beliefs across Mid-Norfolk in the 1920s, he interviewed rural residents in various parishes. An informant from the small parish of Brandiston began his account of witchcraft with the resolute declaration: "We all know there are witches. It is in the Bible. There was the Witch of Endor." This testimony demonstrated the remarkable persistence of traditional folk belief in rural Norfolk, where biblical precedent and local legend continued to reinforce the idea of witches long after formal witch trials had ceased.

#location
Brandiston village, Norfolk, UK.

#upstream-source
M.R. Taylor, "Norfolk Folklore", *Folklore* 40, no. 2 (1929): 113–133.

#title
The Execution of Vicar John Lewis

#summary
John Lewis, the elderly vicar of Brandiston who had served his parish for nearly fifty years, was accused of witchcraft and executed as a wizard during the mid-seventeenth-century witch trials.

#details
John Lewis was presented to the vicarage of Brandiston, Norfolk, on May 6, 1596, and lived there ministering to his parishioners for about half a century. However, during the turbulent years of the English Civil War and the height of the witch-hunting mania propagated by self-styled witch-finders, the elderly clergyman fell under suspicion of sorcery. Subjected to harsh interrogations, Lewis was accused of having familiar spirits or imps, one of which allegedly goaded him into sending a curse that sank a ship at sea. Despite his age and long service, and defending himself courageously at his trial, he was condemned and executed as a wizard, reading the church burial service aloud for himself along the road to the gibbet.

#location
Brandiston parish church (St. Nicholas Church, Brandiston, Norfolk, UK).

#upstream-source
Sir Walter Scott, *Letters on Demonology and Witchcraft* (1830), referencing accounts by Richard Baxter and contemporary records.

#title
The Grey Lady of St. Nicholas

#summary
A spectral Grey Lady is frequently sighted gliding through the churchyard and grounds of old Brandiston Hall, accompanied by sudden drops in temperature and unexplained noises.

#details
Residents and visitors to Brandiston have long reported eerie paranormal encounters centered around the historic Church of St. Nicholas and the surrounding area of old Brandiston Hall. Most notably, a phantom figure known as the "Grey Lady"—believed to be the spirit of a former resident or nun—is seen in the late evening and early morning hours gliding silently through the churchyard. These apparitions are frequently accompanied by an abrupt, unnatural chill and disembodied voices. In addition to the spectral sightings, older cottages in the village have experienced poltergeist phenomena, including doors opening and closing violently, heavy objects moving on their own, and unsettling knocking sounds echoing through the walls.

#location
The ghostly activity is centered around St. Nicholas Church and the grounds of old Brandiston Hall in the rural Norfolk village of Brandiston.
