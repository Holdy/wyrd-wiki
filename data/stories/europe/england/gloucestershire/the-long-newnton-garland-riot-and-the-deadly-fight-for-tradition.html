    The Long Newnton Garland Riot and the Deadly Fight for Tradition

    Summary

    On Trinity Sunday in 1641, the annual procession of a ceremonial garland in the village of Long Newnton erupted into a violent brawl when rival men from Malmesbury attempted to steal it. The fierce competition over the ancient custom persisted for years, culminating in a fatal clash recorded by antiquary John Aubrey around 1660.

    Details

    June 20th, 1641, marked Trinity Sunday in Long Newnton, where villagers maintained an ancient custom of processing an elaborate garland made of willow hoops, greenery, and ribbons to commemorate a royal grant dating back to Saxon times, which culminated in a parish feast. During the afternoon, a crowd of approximately eighty people arrived from nearby Malmesbury intent on seizing the garland. Among the challengers was John Browne, formerly a chimney sweep, dressed in a hobby horse costume with bells on his legs. Taunting the villagers with the cry, "Win it and wear it, come three score of you, but you are but boys to we!", Browne and his companions blocked the procession. A massive and disorderly fight ensued, with the Long Newnton men reportedly getting the worst of the clash. Inter-communal violence over the garland became a recurrent event, with seventeenth-century antiquary John Aubrey later recording that around 1660, a man was actually killed while striving to wrest the garland away.
