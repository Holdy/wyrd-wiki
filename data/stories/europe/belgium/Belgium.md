#title
The Curse of the Chapel of Oeil

#summary
A local man discovers witches conducting rituals inside the isolated Chapel of Oeil, leading to an exorcism that results in a vengeful curse cast upon any dwelling built from the sacred chapel's salvaged stones. 

#details
In the hamlet of Oeil, near Tintange in the Belgian Ardennes, an old mill and a small chapel stood by the Sûre river. On full moon nights, local miller's hand Gaspard witnessed sinister lights and heard strange incantations coming from within the chapel, discovering that a coven of local witches (*macrales*) had been using the holy site to perform dark rituals and cast hexes upon travelers and livestock. Terrified, Gaspard alerted the village clergy. A group of priests converged on the chapel and successfully expelled the witches through a powerful exorcism involving holy water and intense prayer. As they fled in fury, the elder witch pronounced a bitter curse over the sacred ground and its materials, declaring that any structure built using stones taken from the chapel would forever be haunted by restlessness, strange sounds, and misfortune. True to her word, a house later built partly from the chapel's salvaged stones fell permanently under this lingering legacy of bad luck.

#location
The hamlet of Oeil is located near Tintange in the municipality of Fauvillers in the Belgian province of Luxembourg, close to the border with Luxembourg and the Sûre river.

#upstream-source
Walloon folklore, collected as part of the legends circuit of the Anlier Forest, Fauvillers.

#title
The Phantom Nun of Kloosterstraat

#summary
A medieval nun punished for a forbidden romance haunts a historic city street with phantom footsteps and shadowy apparitions. Witnesses along the thoroughfare report hearing her lingering steps and catching glimpses of her spirit near the former monastery site.

#details
In medieval Antwerp, a young nun broke her sacred vows by entering into a romance with a soldier. When their forbidden affair was uncovered, she was severely punished by being walled up alive within the monastery walls. Centuries later, locals and workers along Kloosterstraat continue to experience her lingering presence, frequently hearing her phantom footsteps echoing through the night and occasionally sighting her shadowy form wandering near where the old monastery once stood.

#location
The haunting occurs along Kloosterstraat, a well-known street located in the heart of Antwerp, Belgium.

#title
The Possession at Spanjaardstraat

#summary
During a 19th-century spiritualist investigation at a historic house in Bruges, a visiting medium was violently possessed by the earthbound entity of a 15th-century monk. The possessing spirit re-enacted a heinous murder he had committed centuries prior within the building's walls.

#details
In the late 15th century, a convent stood along the Spanjaardstraat in Bruges, connected by subterranean passages to a nearby Augustinian monastery. An Italian monk fell desperately in love with a young nun named Hortense Dupont. When she steadfastly refused to break her religious vows, the enraged monk stalked her through the convent and stabbed her to death, concealing her body in the tunnels below. Over the centuries, the building became widely known as the *Spookhuis* (Ghost House), haunted by the spectral apparitions of both the murdered nun and the grim monk. In 1890, an English couple living in the house hosted the famous spiritualist medium William Eglinton and novelist Florence Marryat for a series of séances. Upon stepping inside the house, Eglinton was suddenly seized by a demonic and tormenting entity. With a diabolical expression, he ran about shouting and mumbling strange words before collapsing. Upon regaining consciousness, he spoke in an altered voice recounting the centuries-old murder, confessing the crime through the medium's lips to help the tormented soul find release.

#location
The key location is the historic house situated at Spanjaardstraat 17 in Bruges, Belgium, located on the corner next to the Augustinian Bridge.

#upstream-source
Florence Marryat, *There Is No Death* (and her account in the August 29, 1879 issue of *Spiritualist* magazine).
