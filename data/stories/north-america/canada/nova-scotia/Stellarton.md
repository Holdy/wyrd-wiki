#title
The Burnt Mines of Storr Pits

#summary
Following suspected arson and a series of devastating fires and explosions in the 1830s, Stellarton’s earliest coal mine became permanently abandoned and steeped in local lore as the "Burnt Mines."

#details
Opened in 1827 by the General Mining Association on the west bank of the East River, the Storr Pits were Stellarton's very first coal mine. In 1832, a malicious fire broke out underground, killing 14 horses and resulting in an investigation that concluded the blaze was deliberately set by locals opposed to the mining monopoly. After repeated subsequent explosions and fires in 1834, 1836, and 1839, the workings were permanently sealed and abandoned, earning the ominous moniker "The Burnt Mines" as haunting symbols of early industrial turmoil and tragedy.

#location
The Storr Pits site on the west bank of the East River, approximately 200 metres east of the Dorrington Softball Complex in Stellarton, Nova Scotia.

#title
The Prophecies of Mother Coo

#summary
In the late 19th century, a renowned fortune teller accurately predicted major mining disasters in Stellarton and Westville before being condemned as a witch by local clergy.

#details
In 1880, local miners' wives visited Mother Coo—a famous fortune teller and herbalist—who became visibly distressed after reading her cards and tea leaves, prophesying a disastrous flood and loss of life at the Foord Pit in Stellarton. True to her warning, inaccurate surveying led miners to break into water-filled old workings that autumn, followed by a massive methane explosion on November 12, 1880, that killed 44 men. Although she also predicted earlier disasters like the 1873 Drummond Mine explosion, a local priest ultimately denounced her as a witch, making her a feared yet respected figure in local folklore.

#location
The Foord Pit, located on Foord Street in Stellarton, Nova Scotia.
