#title
The Ghost of the Bay of Dead Men

#summary
Local legend holds that Trepassey—historically named *La Baie des Trépassés* or the Bay of Dead Men—is haunted by the souls of shipwreck victims, including a British aristocrat who washed ashore after a fateful storm.

#details
Derived from the French *trépassés* meaning "dead men" or "souls," Trepassey Bay earned its ominous moniker from centuries of devastating shipwrecks along its rocky coast. Among the enduring folklore of the bay is the legend of Lady Mary Alice Tucker, a noblewoman who fled Georgian London with an Irish sailor named Patrick Molloy in 1770 aboard a brig called the *Fanny*. When a white squall shattered their ship near the Grand Banks, the couple washed ashore near Trepassey. In local ghost lore, the bay remains perpetually haunted by the restless spirits of those claimed by its freezing waters, where phantom figures and unearthly cries are said to echo along the shore.

#location
Trepassey Bay on the southeast corner of the Avalon Peninsula, Newfoundland and Labrador.

#title
The Ghost Ship of Trepassey Bay

#summary
Following its tragic sinking in 1881, the vessel *Jane Hunter* became a phantom ship said to haunt the waters of Trepassey Bay, repeatedly appearing as a ghostly silhouette sailing into the harbour at midnight.

#details
In 1881, the ship *Jane Hunter* met its end when it sank in the treacherous waters of Trepassey Bay on the southern shore of Newfoundland. Following the disaster, local fishermen and residents began reporting eerie sightings of the vessel drifting in and out of Trepassey Harbour at midnight. The spectral encounters continued until a local priest finally blessed the wreck site, though according to local legend, ghostly apparitions of the ship still return to glide into the mouth of the harbour during violent storms.

#location
Trepassey Bay and Harbour on the southern shore of the Avalon Peninsula, Newfoundland and Labrador.
