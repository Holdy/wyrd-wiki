#title
The Ghost of Room Six

#summary
Built in 1722, the historic Logan Inn in New Hope, Pennsylvania, is famously haunted by a spirit named Emily and various ghostly phenomena, particularly within Room 6.

#details
Originally opened as the Ferry Tavern in 1727 by John Wells to serve travelers crossing the Delaware River between Pennsylvania and New Jersey, the Logan Inn has accumulated nearly three centuries of history—and plenty of ghostly lore. Guests and staff over the generations have reported doors opening on their own, unexplained footsteps, the phantom scent of lavender, and shadowy figures appearing in mirrors. Most of these paranormal experiences are centered around Room 6, also known as the "Emily Room." While local folklore offers conflicting accounts regarding Emily’s exact identity—varying from a former owner to a matriarch of the family—her lingering presence remains deeply woven into the inn's tradition, alongside sightings of a Revolutionary War soldier and playful children haunting the halls.

#location
The Logan Inn located at 10 West Ferry Street in downtown New Hope, Pennsylvania.

#title
The Handprint of Cell 17

#summary
Following his execution in 1877, a condemned labor activist left a permanent, un-washable muddy handprint on the stone wall of his cell at the Carbon County Jail as a vow of innocence.

#details
In 1877, Alexander Campbell and three other suspected members of the Molly Maguires—a secret society of Irish-American coal miners fighting harsh labor conditions—were convicted of murder on disputed evidence and sentenced to hang at the Carbon County Jail in Mauch Chunk (now Jim Thorpe), Pennsylvania. As guards came to take Campbell to the gallows in the courtyard outside, he vehemently proclaimed his innocence. In a final defiant gesture, he pressed his mud-stained hand against the wall of Cell #17, declaring that the mark would remain forever as proof that he was an innocent man. Despite decades of scrubbing, painting over, and even replacing sections of the wall, the distinct outline of a hand stubbornly reappeared, becoming a legendary local monument to a dark chapter in Pennsylvania's coal mining history.

#location
Cell #17 inside the Old Jail Museum (formerly the Carbon County Jail) located at 128 West Broadway in Jim Thorpe, Pennsylvania.

#title
The Hex Hollow Murder and the Legend of Nelson Rehmeyer

#summary
In 1928, the brutal murder of a Pennsylvania "powwow" healer in his remote York County farmhouse sparked enduring local legends of dark magic, curses, and an unburnable house.

#details
In late November 1928, a superstitious Pennsylvania man named John Blymire, convinced that he had been cursed with crippling health problems and bad luck by a rival faith healer, sought guidance from a local witch named Emma Knopp. Knopp confirmed that Blymire was indeed hexed and identified local farmer and "powwower" Nelson Rehmeyer as the sorcerer responsible. To break the curse, Knopp instructed Blymire to steal Rehmeyer’s copy of the traditional folk-magic spellbook, *Long Lost Friend*, take a lock of his hair, and bury them both deep in the earth. 

On the night of November 27, 1928, Blymire, accompanied by two teenagers named John Curry and Wilbert Hess, broke into Rehmeyer’s weathered, two-story clapboard farmhouse in rural Winterstown, Pennsylvania. When the 60-year-old Rehmeyer refused to surrender his spellbook, a violent confrontation ensued. Blymire choked Rehmeyer while Curry struck him in the head with a block of wood, killing him. Seeking to destroy evidence and lift the curse permanently, the men poured kerosene over Rehmeyer's body and set it on fire before fleeing. 

However, despite the heavy application of fuel, the house and Rehmeyer's body miraculously failed to burn, only smoldering slightly—a phenomenon locals immediately attributed to Rehmeyer's powerful lingering magical protection. The three men were quickly captured, tried, and sentenced to prison, but the farmhouse survived. Today, Rehmeyer's Hollow remains a focal point of Pennsylvania folklore, with locals whispering that the spirit of the murdered healer still haunts his isolated home, where a clock above the stove remains frozen at the exact minute of his death.

#location
Nelson Rehmeyer's farmhouse in Rehmeyer's Hollow near Winterstown, York County, Pennsylvania.

#title
The Hex Hollow Witch Murder

#summary
In 1928, a man convinced he was suffering under an evil curse broke into the farmhouse of a local folk healer to lift the spell, resulting in a brutal murder that became a legendary tale of Pennsylvania Dutch superstition.

#details
In late November 1928, John Blymire, struggling with a lifetime of poor health and bad luck, became convinced that he had been hexed. After consulting multiple practitioners of traditional Pennsylvania Dutch folk magic, known as "powwowing," he visited an elderly witch named Emma Knopp, who identified local farmer and healer Nelson Rehmeyer as the source of the curse. To break the spell, Knopp instructed Blymire to steal Rehmeyer's copy of the magical spellbook, *Long Lost Friend*, and a lock of his hair, and bury them deep underground. Accompanied by two teenage accomplices, John Curry and Wilbert Hess, Blymire drove to Rehmeyer's farmhouse in Winterstown under the light of a full moon on Thanksgiving eve. When Rehmeyer refused to surrender his book, a violent struggle broke out, ending with Rehmeyer being beaten, strangled, and set on fire. The three perpetrators were quickly apprehended, tried, and sentenced to life imprisonment, cementing the event in local lore alongside stories of unburned bodies and clocks frozen forever at the moment of the crime.

#location
The historic two-story farmhouse of Nelson Rehmeyer is located in Rehmeyer's Hollow near Winterstown in York County, Pennsylvania.

#title
The Hex of Rehmeyer's Hollow

#summary
Convinced he had been cursed by a rival Pennsylvania Dutch healer, a troubled practitioner hunted down the purported sorcerer at his isolated farmhouse to lift the hex, resulting in a brutal murder that cemented local folklore. The historic farmhouse remains standing as a tangible relic of Pennsylvania's dark occult history.

#details
In rural York County, Pennsylvania, a local faith healer and practitioner of Pennsylvania Dutch folk magic (known as "powwowing") named John Blymire became convinced that a malevolent hex had been placed upon him, destroying his health, peace of mind, and family life. Believing the curse originated from Nelson Rehmeyer—an older, formidable local powwower and healer living in a secluded valley known as Rehmeyer's Hollow—Blymire and two accomplices sought out Rehmeyer at his isolated stone farmhouse. Desperate to force Rehmeyer to remove the hex and surrender his magical grimoire, *The Long Lost Friend*, the men confronted him in his parlor. During the ensuing violent struggle, Rehmeyer was beaten and strangled to death, and the house was set ablaze in a failed attempt to destroy evidence. Though the fire mysteriously extinguished itself while leaving the structure intact, the sensational "Hex Murder" trial shocked the nation and permanently embedded the dread of supernatural curses and hexes into Pennsylvania Dutch folklore.

#location
The historic Hex House is located on Rehmeyers Hollow Road in North Hopewell Township (near Stewartstown), York County, Pennsylvania.

#title
The Providence Meeting House Legends

#summary
A historic stone chapel and Quaker cemetery near Perryopolis became the focus of intense urban legends during the 1980s involving secret witch trials, hellhounds, and ghostly apparitions.

#details
Situated on Quaker Church Road just outside Perryopolis, the site features a stone chapel built in 1895 atop the ruins of the older Providence Meeting House, which operated from 1793 to 1870. During the Satanic Panic of the 1980s, rumors spread through the region that a secret witch trial and execution had taken place at the meeting house, and that the spirit of the condemned witch—as well as supernatural hellhounds—haunted the grounds. While historical records show no witch trials ever occurred there (Pennsylvania's only recorded witch trial took place under William Penn in 1683 and resulted in acquittals), visitors and folklore enthusiasts continue to share tales of paranormal activity and eerie visual anomalies captured in the chapel windows.

#location
The historic Providence Meeting House chapel and cemetery are located on Quaker Church Road just outside Perryopolis in Fayette County, Pennsylvania.

#title
The Sad Spirit of Swamp Church

#summary
A mournful female ghost and her baby are said to haunt the historic Bethesda Evangelical Church in Centre County, appearing every spring to search for the love denied them.

#details
Built in 1855 along Penns Creek and nicknamed "Swamp Church" due to its boggy surroundings, the Bethesda Evangelical Church in Gregg Township is steeped in local folklore. According to legend, during the 1880s, local residents Jacob and Rebecca Schultz witnessed a shadowy female figure carrying a baby walk down the road and enter the church on May 3. As she approached, the church doors opened and lights flared to life while she softly called out the name "Will." Local tradition holds that the apparition is an unwed mother from the Civil War era whose fiancé, Private William Knarr, was killed in action at the Battle of Chancellorsville on May 3, 1863, and whose baby was rejected by the congregation.

#location
Bethesda Evangelical Church stands along a dirt road near Farmers Mills in Gregg Township, Centre County, Pennsylvania.

#title
The Spirits of the General Wayne Inn

#summary
A pre-Revolutionary War tavern in Merion Station is reputed to be haunted by numerous spirits, including Hessian soldiers and Revolutionary War figures.

#details
Dating back to its origin on land once owned by William Penn, the General Wayne Inn in Merion Station served travelers and patrons for centuries, hosting historical figures like George Washington and Benjamin Franklin. Over the decades, staff and visitors reported numerous paranormal encounters, such as invisible presences blowing on the necks of patrons at the bar, a disembodied head in the kitchen, and a Hessian soldier named Wilhelm lingering in the basement. Local records and historical accounts note sightings of soldier apparitions stretching back over a century, cementing the inn's reputation as one of Pennsylvania's most haunted historic properties.

#location
The former General Wayne Inn is located at 625 Montgomery Avenue in Merion Station, Pennsylvania.

#title
The Suscon Screamer

#summary
Travelers driving along a winding backroad in Luzerne County report encountering a ghostly woman in white who vanishes after letting out a terrifying scream. According to local lore, she appears if motorists stop and honk their horns near the site of an old railroad underpass.

#details
For generations, motorists traveling along Suscon Road in Pittston Township have shared eerie accounts of a phantom apparition known as the Suscon Screamer. Legend dictates that if a driver stops near the former railroad underpass and honks their horn three times, a pale woman in white manifests in the rearview mirror or drifts through the nearby trees before uttering a bloodcurdling shriek and disappearing into the dark. While some mythologies attribute the phantom to a jilted bride, a runaway, or a tragic crash victim, local folklore became deeply intertwined with the grim 1969 murder of fifteen-year-old Patricia Emlaw, whose body was discovered in the nearby woods. Today, visitors still report hearing disembodied screams and spotting a spectral figure along this isolated stretch of Pennsylvania pavement.

#location
The haunting takes place along Suscon Road in Pittston Township, Luzerne County, Pennsylvania, specifically near the site of the former railroad underpass in the 1000 block of the road.
