#title
The Apparition of Room 24 at Concord's Colonial Inn

#summary
Room 24 of Concord's Colonial Inn—a historic building that once served as a makeshift Revolutionary War hospital—has been famously haunted by a shadowy gray apparition since 1966.

#details
During the American Revolution, parts of the historic structure that now make up Concord's Colonial Inn were used as a medical facility where wounded soldiers were treated and many perished. The building's paranormal reputation gained widespread attention in 1966 when honeymooners M.P. and Judith Fellenz checked into Room 24 on the second floor. In the dead of night, Mrs. Fellenz awoke to find a grayish, shadowy figure standing beside the bed before it drifted toward the fireplace and vanished. In the decades following this first documented sighting, guests and staff have reported numerous other hauntings, including phantom soldiers, children's footsteps in the hallways, and restless spirits moving through the historic inn.

#location
Concord's Colonial Inn at 48 Monument Square in Concord, Massachusetts.

#title
The Curse of the Tree of Knowledge

#summary
Local folklore dictates that the town of Duxbury will be cursed if a historic crossroads marker commemorating the colonial "Tree of Knowledge" is ever removed or forgotten. After a lightning strike destroyed the ancient oak tree where mail was once left, its spirit appeared in a dream demanding that the exact spot be permanently marked.

#details
In the late 17th century, at a remote fork in the road in the southwestern section of Duxbury, Massachusetts (where the old Boston-to-Plymouth highway met the road leading to Duxbury village), post riders dropped off mail in a box nailed to an ancient oak tree. Townspeople affectionately nicknamed it the "Tree of Knowledge." According to local tradition and a dramatic poem written in the early 20th century by local selectman Wendell B. Phillips, a violent midnight lightning storm struck and shattered the tree. Shortly after, a local resident dreamed of the tree's spirit, which warned that the spot must be marked and remembered or else a curse would befall the town. Generations of residents have faithfully maintained a succession of markers at the crossroads—from painted wooden signs to a granite monument installed during the 1937 Tercentenary Celebration—to ensure the curse is kept at bay.

#location
The historic crossroads where Elm Street meets South Street (near Route 53) in the Tarkiln neighborhood of Duxbury, Massachusetts.

#upstream-source
Justin Winsor, *History of the Town of Duxbury* (1849); Wendell B. Phillips, private dramatic poem on the Tree of Knowledge (c. 1915).

#title
The Ghost of the Eunice Williams Covered Bridge

#summary
The ghost of Eunice Williams, a young mother killed during the tragic 1704 Deerfield Massacre, is said to haunt the covered bridge spanning the Green River where she breathed her last.

#details
On February 29, 1704, during Queen Anne's War, combined French and Native forces attacked the frontier settlement of Deerfield, Massachusetts, killing dozens of residents and taking over a hundred captives on a grueling winter march to Canada. Among the captured was Eunice Williams, wife of the town's minister, who had given birth just a day prior. Weakened and unable to keep up with the column of prisoners, Eunice collapsed near the Green River and was struck down. Over the centuries, locals and travelers have reported encountering her sorrowful spirit haunting the riverbanks and the covered bridge that now bears her name, endlessly searching for her family.

#location
The Eunice Williams Covered Bridge spanning the Green River in Greenfield, Massachusetts.

#title
The Ghostly Belle of the Wayside Inn

#summary
Room 9 of Longfellow's Wayside Inn in Sudbury is said to be haunted by Jerusha Howe, a 19th-century innkeeper's sister who died of a broken heart after her English fiancé failed to return.

#details
Jerusha Howe lived during the early 19th century and managed Howe's Tavern (later known as Longfellow's Wayside Inn) alongside her brother Lyman. Revered as the "belle of Sudbury" for her refinement, musical talents, and grace, Jerusha reportedly fell in love with a visitor from England who promised to return for her but never did. She never married, spent much of her life confined to her room above the old kitchen, and eventually passed away from tuberculosis in 1842. Ever since, guests staying in Room 9—often nicknamed the Hobgoblin Room—have reported paranormal occurrences, including phantom footsteps half-running and half-floating across the room, cold spots, and encounters with the spirit of Jerusha sitting by the fireplace or near her old chair. Visitors continue the tradition of leaving handwritten notes tucked into the room's ceiling joists and hidden drawers for the spectral belle.

#location
Longfellow's Wayside Inn is located at 72 Wayside Inn Road in Sudbury, Massachusetts.

#title
The Ghosts of the Barnstable House

#summary
Relocated to Cape Cod in the 18th century, the historic Barnstable House is reputed to harbor up to eleven spirits, including a young girl who drowned in the basement well and a former sea captain.

#details
Originally built in Scituate in 1713 and reassembled on Main Street in Barnstable in 1716, the Barnstable House has a long history intertwined with local folklore. Over the centuries, occupants and visitors have reported encounters with up to eleven different ghosts haunting the premises. Among the most prominent tales is that of a young child named Lucy, who according to legend drowned in a well located in the cellar. Other frequent apparitions include a woman in white and a former sea captain who once resided in the home. Paranormal occurrences documented at the site include full-bodied manifestations, phantom footsteps, unexplained knocking, and sudden, intense cold spots.

#location
The Barnstable House is located at 3010 Main Street in Barnstable, Massachusetts.

#title
The Ghosts of the Bloody Pit

#summary
Excavated through the Berkshire Hills over a tumultuous 25-year period, the Hoosac Tunnel claimed the lives of over two hundred workers and became infamous for reports of headless apparitions, agonized phantom moans, and supernatural rescues. Following gruesome industrial accidents and an unsolved murder within the damp shaft, engineers, workers, and visitors experienced chilling encounters with spectral lights and vengeful spirits deep inside the mountain.

#details
Beginning in 1851, the construction of the 4.75-mile Hoosac Tunnel through Hoosac Mountain in western Massachusetts was plagued by explosions, cave-ins, and toxic gas leaks, resulting in more than 200 fatalities and earning the passage the grim nickname of the "Bloody Pit." In March 1865, a nitroglycerin blast engineered by workers Ned Brinkman, Billy Nash, and Ringo Kelley resulted in Brinkman and Nash being buried alive under tons of rock. A year later, Kelley's strangled corpse was discovered near the same spot, fueling persistent rumors that he had been murdered by the vengeful spirits of his coworkers. Throughout construction and subsequent decades of operation, numerous visitors reported paranormal activity inside the dark, echoing shaft. In September 1868, mechanical engineer Paul Travers investigated worker complaints and heard the clear, agonizing groans of a man in pain echoing from the pitch-black interior when no other people were present. In June 1872, Dr. Clifford J. Owen and operations superintendent James McKinstrey witnessed a headless, human-shaped blue light hovering toward them before vanishing into the stonework. Later railway employees, such as track maintainer Joseph Impoco, credited a disembodied voice with warning him just in time to escape an oncoming train and a high-voltage electrical arc. To this day, local legends persist regarding the haunting sounds and ghostly figures that linger along the tracks.

#location
The Hoosac Tunnel is a historic railroad tunnel piercing through Hoosac Mountain in western Massachusetts, located near the city of North Adams.

#title
The Phantoms of the Hoosac Tunnel

#summary
Carved through the Berkshire Mountains under deadly conditions that claimed nearly two hundred lives, the Hoosac Tunnel became notorious for ghostly moans, blue lights, and headless apparitions.

#details
Construction on the 4.75-mile Hoosac Tunnel began in 1851 and quickly became known as the "Bloody Pit" due to dangerous explosives, cave-ins, and toxic gases that killed approximately 190 workers. In 1865, a nitroglycerin explosion under suspicious circumstances killed two workers, after which a third explosives expert, Ringo Kelley, was found strangled to death deep within the shaft. Following these tragedies, laborers refused to work after dark, reporting chilling cries of agony and phantom sounds echoing through the dark tunnel. In 1868 and later in 1872, investigators and engineers—including a doctor who diagnosed the tunnel with "poltergeist-itis"—reported witnessing a glowing, blue, headless apparition floating along the tunnel floor before vanishing into thin air. Even today, as an active railroad tunnel, travelers and workers continue to report strange presences and muffled voices in the dark.

#location
The Hoosac Tunnel cuts through the Hoosac Mountain Range in Berkshire County, Massachusetts, with its East Portal located in Florida, Massachusetts, and its West Portal in North Adams, Massachusetts.

#title
The Red-Headed Hitchhiker of Route 44

#summary
A malicious red-haired phantom haunts a dark stretch of road in Massachusetts. The spirit terrorizes unsuspecting motorists before vanishing into thin air.

#details
After mocking local ghost tales over dinner, a disbelieving couple decides to drive home along Route 44 late at night. Suddenly, a bushy-bearded man with red hair, a plaid shirt, and blue jeans appears running alongside their car, leering through the passenger window. When the terrified driver speeds up, the phantom instantly reappears inside the back seat, tormenting them with maniacal laughter. As the panicked wife begins reciting the rosary, the entity grimaces at the sacred words and vanishes without a trace.

#location
Route 44 along the border of Seekonk and Rehoboth, Massachusetts.

#title
The Tragedy and Haunting of Houghton Mansion

#summary
Following a horrific automobile accident and subsequent suicides and deaths in 1914, the grand Houghton Mansion in North Adams became plagued by paranormal phenomena, including shadow figures, disembodied voices, and lingering sorrow.

#details
In August 1914, wealthy industrialist and first mayor of North Adams, Albert Charles (A.C.) Houghton, set out on a drive in his Pierce-Arrow touring car with his daughter Mary, friends, and their long-time chauffeur, John Widders. While navigating a hill under repair, the car plunged down an embankment, instantly killing their friend Sybil Hutton and fatally injuring Mary Houghton. Stricken with intense grief and guilt, the chauffeur John Widders shot himself in the mansion's stable just hours later. A.C. Houghton himself passed away nine days after the accident, deeply broken by the tragedy. Since these events, visitors and paranormal investigators have reported frequent haunting phenomena throughout the 17-room neoclassical estate, including shadow figures, weeping apparitions of Mary and A.C., ghostly footsteps on the stairs, and disembodied voices demanding intruders leave.

#location
The Houghton Mansion is located at 172 Church Street in North Adams, Massachusetts.
