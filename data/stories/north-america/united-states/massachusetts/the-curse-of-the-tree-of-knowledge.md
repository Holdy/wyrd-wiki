#title
The Curse of the Tree of Knowledge

#summary
Local folklore dictates that the town of Duxbury will be cursed if a historic crossroads marker commemorating the colonial "Tree of Knowledge" is ever removed or forgotten. After a lightning strike destroyed the ancient oak tree where mail was once left, its spirit appeared in a dream demanding that the exact spot be permanently marked.

#details
In the late 17th century, at a remote fork in the road in the southwestern section of Duxbury, Massachusetts (where the old Boston-to-Plymouth highway met the road leading to Duxbury village), post riders dropped off mail in a box nailed to an ancient oak tree. Townspeople affectionately nicknamed it the "Tree of Knowledge." According to local tradition and a dramatic poem written in the early 20th century by local selectman Wendell B. Phillips, a violent midnight lightning storm struck and shattered the tree. Shortly after, a local resident dreamed of the tree's spirit, which warned that the spot must be marked and remembered or else a curse would befall the town. Generations of residents have faithfully maintained a succession of markers at the crossroads—from painted wooden signs to a granite monument installed during the 1937 Tercentenary Celebration—to ensure the curse is kept at bay.

#location
The historic crossroads where Elm Street meets South Street (near Route 53) in the Tarkiln neighborhood of Duxbury, Massachusetts.
