The House of Blue Lights

Summary

Local legend long claimed that an eccentric Indianapolis millionaire kept his deceased wife's body in a glass coffin surrounded by blue Christmas lights in his secluded estate. In truth, the wealthy inventor Skiles Test simply loved the color blue and used blue bug-zapping and holiday lights, though trespassers and urban explorers propagated the macabre folklore for decades.

Details

Skiles Edward Test, a wealthy inventor and businessman, lived on a large, heavily wooded farmstead off Fall Creek Road on the northeast side of Indianapolis. Fuelled by his technical ingenuity and vast inheritance from his father (president of National Motor Vehicle Company), Test built private rail systems, his own power plant, and an elaborate swimming pool on the estate. Because Test was fond of the color blue—using blue holiday lights and blue bug-zapping lights around his property—local teenagers and outsiders spun wild rumors between the world wars. The core legend insisted that his wife had died in a tragic accident and that he kept her corpse preserved in a glass casket illuminated by blue lights. Despite no such tragedy occurring, the property became plagued by trespassers and vandals throughout the 1950s and 1960s, leading Test to erect tall fences and eventually stay elsewhere at night. Test passed away in 1964, and though the estate was later razed and turned into Skiles Test Nature Park, local folklore persists that blue lights can still be seen flickering through the woods at night.
