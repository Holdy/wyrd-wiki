The Ghosts of the Bloody Pit

Summary

Excavated through the Berkshire Hills over a tumultuous 25-year period, the Hoosac Tunnel claimed the lives of over two hundred workers and became infamous for reports of headless apparitions, agonized phantom moans, and supernatural rescues. Following gruesome industrial accidents and an unsolved murder within the damp shaft, engineers, workers, and visitors experienced chilling encounters with spectral lights and vengeful spirits deep inside the mountain.

Details

Beginning in 1851, the construction of the 4.75-mile Hoosac Tunnel through Hoosac Mountain in western Massachusetts was plagued by explosions, cave-ins, and toxic gas leaks, resulting in more than 200 fatalities and earning the passage the grim nickname of the "Bloody Pit." In March 1865, a nitroglycerin blast engineered by workers Ned Brinkman, Billy Nash, and Ringo Kelley resulted in Brinkman and Nash being buried alive under tons of rock. A year later, Kelley's strangled corpse was discovered near the same spot, fueling persistent rumors that he had been murdered by the vengeful spirits of his coworkers. Throughout construction and subsequent decades of operation, numerous visitors reported paranormal activity inside the dark, echoing shaft. In September 1868, mechanical engineer Paul Travers investigated worker complaints and heard the clear, agonizing groans of a man in pain echoing from the pitch-black interior when no other people were present. In June 1872, Dr. Clifford J. Owen and operations superintendent James McKinstrey witnessed a headless, human-shaped blue light hovering toward them before vanishing into the stonework. Later railway employees, such as track maintainer Joseph Impoco, credited a disembodied voice with warning him just in time to escape an oncoming train and a high-voltage electrical arc. To this day, local legends persist regarding the haunting sounds and ghostly figures that linger along the tracks.
