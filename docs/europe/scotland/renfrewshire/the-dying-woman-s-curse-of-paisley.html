The Dying Woman’s Curse of Paisley

Summary

Condemned for witchcraft in 1697, Agnes Naismith placed a generational curse on the town of Paisley, which local folklore claimed was kept at bay only by an upside-down horseshoe sealing their mass grave at Maxwellton Crossroads until roadworks disturbed it in the 1960s.

Details

In 1697, following the accusations of a young girl named Christian Shaw, seven people were convicted of witchcraft in the town of Paisley, Renfrewshire. Six were publicly hanged and burned at the Gallow Green on June 10, 1697, marking the last mass execution for witchcraft in Western Europe. As she faced her execution, an elderly woman named Agnes Naismith cast a fierce dying curse upon all those present and their descendants, declaring that misfortune would plague them.

To contain the malice of the dead witches, their remains were buried at Maxwellton Crossroads and the site was sealed underneath an upside-down iron horseshoe. Local folklore maintained that as long as the horseshoe remained undisturbed, the curse would stay at bay. For generations, any major tragedy or misfortune in Paisley was blamed on Naismith's curse. In the 1960s, roadworks disturbed the grave and removed the horseshoe, after which local legend tied the subsequent economic decline of the town to the unhindered curse.
