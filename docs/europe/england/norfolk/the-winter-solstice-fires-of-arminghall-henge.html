    The Winter Solstice Fires of Arminghall Henge

    Summary

    Discovered from the air in 1929, the prehistoric timber circle of Arminghall Henge served as a monumental gathering place where ancient communities celebrated the winter solstice by aligning with Chapel Hill and ultimately consuming the massive wooden structure in a great ceremonial fire.

    Details

    Situated in a meadow on a flood bank of the River Tas in Arminghall, Norfolk, Arminghall Henge was first spotted as circular cropmarks from the air in 1929 by Wing Commander Gilbert Insall. Subsequent archaeological excavations revealed a 4,000-year-old Neolithic monument consisting of a double-ditch earthwork system enclosing a horseshoe-shaped timber circle made of huge tree trunks standing up to 10 metres tall. Local lore and archaeological study describe the site as a sacred ceremonial focal point for winter solstice gatherings. The monument was precisely aligned so that the setting midwinter sun appeared to roll down the slope of nearby Chapel Hill like a ball of fire into the river below, offering ancient peoples hope after the longest nights of the year. Folklore and research suggest that toward the end of its active use, the monumental timber posts were deliberately set ablaze during a massive winter gathering, creating an enormous bonfire that burned for days before the site faded into local memory.
